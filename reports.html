Vulnerability-assessment reporting

Example report findings

These examples demonstrate how a technical observation can be translated into evidence, business risk and a practical remediation path for a small organisation.

Fictional demonstration material

These are not findings from a CyberAidAU client engagement and do not describe a real organisation. CyberAidAU remains in research and development and is not currently trading. Names, systems, evidence and scenarios below are deliberately fictional.

What a useful finding should contain

  • A clear title and severity
  • Scope and evidence that can be checked
  • A plain-English explanation of business impact
  • Prioritised remediation and verification steps

What these examples do not prove

A sample report demonstrates communication and reporting structure. It is not evidence that a real client assessment occurred, and it must not be used as a substitute for written authorisation, scoped testing or organisation-specific analysis.

Illustrative findings

Administrative accounts do not require multifactor authentication

Example reference: CA-EX-001

High

Example observation

The fictional administration portal accepted a username and password without requiring a second authentication factor.

Why it matters

A stolen or reused password could provide direct access to business settings, customer information and privileged functions.

Recommended action

  • Require phishing-resistant MFA for every privileged account.
  • Remove shared administrator accounts.
  • Review recent sign-ins and recovery methods.

Verification

Confirm each administrator is challenged for MFA during a fresh sign-in and that bypass or legacy authentication paths are disabled.

Internet-accessible management interface

Example reference: CA-EX-002

High

Example observation

A fictional firewall-management page was reachable from the public internet rather than being restricted to an approved administration network.

Why it matters

Public management interfaces increase exposure to password attacks, product vulnerabilities and unauthorised configuration changes.

Recommended action

  • Restrict management access to a VPN or allowlisted addresses.
  • Patch the appliance and require MFA.
  • Review configuration and authentication logs.

Verification

Retest from an untrusted network and confirm the management service is no longer reachable.

Unsupported public website component

Example reference: CA-EX-003

High

Example observation

A fictional public website disclosed a component version that had reached end of support and no longer received security fixes.

Why it matters

Attackers commonly search for known weaknesses in outdated internet-facing software. A successful compromise could affect the website, customer data or hosting account.

Recommended action

  • Back up and upgrade to a supported release.
  • Remove unused themes, plugins and accounts.
  • Check hosting and application logs for suspicious activity.

Verification

Confirm the running version after the upgrade and verify that the obsolete component is no longer externally identifiable.

Email domain lacks an enforced anti-spoofing policy

Example reference: CA-EX-004

Medium

Example observation

The fictional business domain published SPF and DKIM records, but its DMARC policy requested monitoring only rather than quarantine or rejection.

Why it matters

Criminals may have more opportunity to impersonate the business in phishing and invoice-fraud messages.

Recommended action

  • Review DMARC reports and legitimate senders.
  • Correct SPF and DKIM alignment issues.
  • Move gradually to quarantine and then rejection.

Verification

Check DNS, send controlled test messages and confirm legitimate services authenticate and align correctly before enforcement is increased.

Former staff account remains active

Example reference: CA-EX-005

Medium

Example observation

A fictional cloud account belonging to a departed staff member remained enabled and retained access to shared business files.

Why it matters

Unused accounts can preserve access tokens, recovery paths and permissions that may be abused without being noticed promptly.

Recommended action

  • Disable the account and revoke active sessions and tokens.
  • Transfer required business records to an accountable owner.
  • Introduce a documented departure checklist.

Verification

Confirm the account cannot authenticate, tokens have been revoked and required records remain available to authorised staff.
